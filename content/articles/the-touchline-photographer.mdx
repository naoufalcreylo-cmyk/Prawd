*The speaker is invented. Think of them as the person in a wet coat you've seen at the corner flag, shooting a game no newspaper is covering.*

<Q>How does someone end up photographing non-league football?</Q>

By accident, like most things at this level. I took a camera along to watch my nephew, and the club secretary saw it and asked if I could do the team picture. That was the start. Nobody's ever told me to stop.

<Q>What's the hardest part?</Q>

You're always in the wrong place. A professional at a big ground has the angles worked out, but I've got one pair of legs and a pitch that's a hundred metres long. Whichever end I pick, the goal goes in at the other one. I've probably got more pictures of defenders looking disappointed than anyone in the county.

<Q>Does the equipment matter much?</Q>

Less than people think. A long lens helps, and a camera that can see when it gets dark at four o'clock in November. But knowing the game helps more. If you can tell that the left back always overlaps, you can be ready for it. The autofocus doesn't know that.

<Q>What do you look for that isn't the action?</Q>

The bits around the edge. The substitute holding the water bottles. The linesman who's also somebody's dad. The tea hatch. Those are the pictures people ask for years later. Nobody remembers the score, but they want to see the old stand, and the bloke who used to run the raffle.

<Q>Who are you actually taking the pictures for?</Q>

The players, mainly. Most of them have never had a good photograph of themselves playing. You send a picture across and a thirty-five-year-old centre half gets all shy about it and asks if he can use it on his phone. That's as good as it gets, really.

<Q>And the one you'd save if there was a fire?</Q>

A team photo. It's never the best-looking thing, because somebody's always blinking. But I've done enough of them now to see people get older in the back row, and to see some of them stop appearing. A team photo looks like nothing when you take it. Give it twenty years.
