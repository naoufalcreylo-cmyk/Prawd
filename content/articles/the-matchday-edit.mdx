{/* section: the-neighbourhood */}

For most of the week, a football ground is an odd neighbour: an enormous building with nothing happening in it, wedged among houses that were often built for the same workforce it once entertained.

The relationship isn't always gentle. The street above runs through Anfield in Liverpool, photographed in 2013 with its houses boarded up ahead of demolition. Grounds and their streets change each other, sometimes over generations and sometimes all at once.

<Figure id="craven-cottage-stevenage-road" />

On Stevenage Road in Fulham, the long brick side of Craven Cottage simply continues the street. On a January afternoon with nobody around, you could almost walk past it.

{/* section: the-journey */}

A matchday begins well before the ground comes into view. In April 1975, the diesel locomotive above was hauling a football special back from the FA Trophy Final at Wembley, passing the site of Brixworth station in Northamptonshire. Trains like this could carry a town's away support in one go.

The train has often given way to the coach and the car. But the journey is still where people stop being individuals with jobs and start being, for an afternoon, part of an away end. You see it in the cover photograph of this feature too: Aston Villa fans at Wembley in 2018, walking in the same direction.

{/* section: the-rituals */}

Before the 2018 Championship play-off final, Aston Villa supporters gathered at the Windermere, a pub near Wembley. There are club shirts, shared tables and plenty of time. Nothing has happened yet, and for some people this will turn out to be the best part of the day.

<Figure id="windermere-pub-kids" />

Round the side of the same pub, the children have found a patch of grass and started a game of their own. It's a small thing to notice, and it comes up on matchdays almost everywhere. The adults have come to watch football. The kids can't wait, so they play.

{/* section: the-stands */}

Then it's time. At the Schützenwiese in Winterthur, on a September night in 2022, floodlights hang over a pitch that looks almost too green, and the far stand is lost in pale smoke during a fan choreography.

<Figure id="northern-guard-smoke" />

At Keyworth Stadium in Hamtramck, Michigan, the Northern Guard Supporters turn the same idea into green, pink and yellow. Pyrotechnics are banned at many grounds, and they can be dangerous. As a visual record, though, these pictures show something plain enough. For a while, the people watching become the thing to look at.

{/* section: the-aftermath */}

Nobody photographs this part much. Seen from above, the crowd leaving Fratton Park in Portsmouth stops being a crowd and becomes traffic, spreading out through the junctions until it's gone. An hour later, you'd never know.

<Figure id="villa-royal-route" />

The same Aston Villa supporters who walked up to Wembley earlier in this feature walk back down the Royal Route after losing the final 1-0 to Fulham. The crowd is the same size and the colours are the same, but the pace is different. Whichever way it went, the walk home is the moment when a matchday turns into something you'll remember, or try not to.
