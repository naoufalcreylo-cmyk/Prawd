*The speaker is invented. Most villages have someone like this, who you'll usually find out on the field on a weekday, when no one else is looking at it.*

<Q>How long have you been looking after the pitch?</Q>

Long enough to have opinions about it. I took over from a man who'd been doing it for thirty years, and he gave me a key, a line marker and a list of who not to trust. All three have been useful.

<Q>What don't people understand about the job?</Q>

That it's mostly drainage. People see the stripes, and the stripes take an hour. They don't see the rest of it. Football's really just something that happens on top of a drainage problem. I've learned more about what's under that field than I know about my own family.

<Q>What's your biggest enemy?</Q>

Rain, then moles, then anyone who thinks a wet pitch is fine for "just a quick kickabout". Then the dogs. I've got nothing against dogs. I've got something against the people who act like they didn't see what the dog did.

<Q>Do the players ever thank you?</Q>

Now and then. Mostly they tell me that the goalmouth's bumpy, as if I'd done it on purpose. I don't mind. If the pitch is good, nobody says anything. That's how you know you've got it right. It's a bit like being a referee, except you can go home earlier.

<Q>What's your favourite time on the pitch?</Q>

Early on a Saturday, with the lines freshly done and nobody on it. It looks perfect for about four hours, and then twenty-two people come and ruin it. And that's as it should be. It's what it's for.

<Q>What happens when you stop?</Q>

Somebody else will get the key. It's only ever a lend, a field like this. It was here before me, and with any luck it'll have a game on it long after. I just hope they keep off the middle in February.
