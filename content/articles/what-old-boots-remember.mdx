The pair of Gola Speedsters on this page, probably made in England in the 1970s, have seen better days. The leather has darkened and creased, the moulded studs on the sole are chipped and pale, and the yellow stripes have gone a little dull. They're also an elegant piece of design, low and slim in a way that a modern boot isn't.

Old boots are good at this. Hold one up and you can read a surprising amount about the way football used to be played, and about how it felt.

## From armour to slipper

Go back far enough and a football boot is closer to a work boot. A pair from the 1920s, displayed in the museum at the Arena AufSchalke in Gelsenkirchen, is a hefty object: dark leather with pale panels, cut high around the ankle, with wide stripes across the upper. Boots like these were built to protect against heavy balls and heavy tackles on heavy ground, and they took on water and weight as a game went on.

<Figure id="schalke-1920s-boots" />

Over the following decades the boot became progressively lighter and lower. There's nothing mysterious about why. Faster, drier and better-kept pitches rewarded quick feet, and the players whose feet were quickest wanted less leather in the way. By the 1970s, a boot like the Gola had most of the shape we'd recognise today.

## The sole of the matter

Turn a boot over and you'll find the most practical part of its history. Screw-in studs, moulded studs and then blades have each promised more grip for less risk. The Nike pair below combines metal and plastic studs, a set-up familiar from wet pitches for years. On the underside, football's arguments about traction and safety are written in hardware.

<Figure id="nike-studs" />

The modern boot takes all of that and adds a layer of showmanship. Today's are bright, light, synthetic, and designed to be seen from the top of a stand. They're engineered to a degree that would astonish anyone lacing up leather in the 1920s, and they come in a new colourway every few months.

## The shape of a foot

Part of what makes old boots interesting is that they're more than a design record. Leather takes on the shape of its owner. A boot that's been worn for years is a cast of one particular pair of feet, with the bulges, the creases and the worn patch from a favourite way of striking a ball. It remembers, in a very literal way.

Modern synthetics don't do this nearly as well, and they rarely last long enough to try. That's a loss, if a minor one. Next time you find a dried-out pair at the back of a garage, don't be too quick to throw them away. Knock the mud off and have a look. Somebody once played in those.
