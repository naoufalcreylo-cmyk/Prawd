Ask a football supporter whether wearing the same socks affects the result, and they'll laugh and tell you of course not. Then ask them to change the socks.

Every club's support runs on small, private systems of rules. Some go in through the same turnstile. Others buy the same thing from the same van, or take the long route past the same corner because they took it the day of a famous win. Plenty of these habits began as a joke, and most have outlasted the people they were first shared with. Few would call any of it belief, and yet not many supporters would risk stopping.

## Rehearsing belonging

Anthropologists would recognise all this immediately. Rituals are less about getting a result than about marking time and belonging to a group. Football supplies a schedule of them: a regular day, a known route, a fixed sequence that gradually builds from ordinary life to the moment of kick-off. The match is the event, but it's the walk to the ground that does much of the work of turning a person into part of a crowd.

You can watch this transformation happen on any street near a stadium. A few people in club colours become a trickle. The trickle becomes a stream. By the time it gets to the ground, the mood, the pace and even the volume of conversation have changed. Nobody organised it, and nobody needs to.

## The raised scarf

Some rituals are much larger. A section of supporters holding up scarves before kick-off is among football's most familiar images, and it's an unusually precise piece of collective behaviour. There's no conductor. Thousands of people do the same thing at the same moment, on a cue they all understand, for the benefit of nobody in particular.

In the photograph at the top of this page, supporters of FC Cincinnati in The Bailey, the club's main fan section, hold up their scarves in 2016. The fact that the club was then only in its first season says something about how quickly these habits now travel. Supporter culture has become a shared international language, with its own vocabulary of scarves, flags, drums and songs, and a new club's fans can pick it up and make it theirs almost immediately.

## What gets passed down

The most lasting rituals are the ones handed on. A parent decides when a child is old enough for a first match, and that decision alone carries some weight. They show the child where to stand, when to sing and whom to dislike. Years later, that child may be taking somebody else along, repeating the same instructions and, without really noticing, the same route.

This is why changes around the edges of the matchday can provoke such strong feeling. Moving a kick-off time, relocating a ground or closing the old approach to the stadium might look minor on paper. Each one breaks a chain of small repetitions that many people have spent their lives building.

## Keeping the socks

Supporters are rarely fools about any of this. They know that their team's fortunes are set by money, injuries, tactics and chance. They know that the socks don't matter. The ritual doesn't change the outcome, and in some ways that's what makes it so useful. A season is largely beyond anyone's control, but the way you go to the match is entirely yours.

So you take the long way round, stand in your usual place, and hold up the scarf when everybody else does. Then, when the final whistle goes, whatever the score, you've at least done your part properly.
