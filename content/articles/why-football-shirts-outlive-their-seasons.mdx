Very little in football is built to last. Managers don't, and neither do squads. A stadium may go after a century, and the record of a season is overwritten the following August. Yet the most disposable product in the sport, a polyester top released on a commercial cycle and superseded within twelve months, turns out to be the thing people refuse to throw away.

Walk through the Feria de Tristán Narvaja, the Sunday street market in Montevideo, and you can see it on the rails. Shirts hang by the hundred, in a mix of clubs and countries that no official shop would ever put side by side. Nobody selling them is asking which season you consider current. In a market like this, every shirt is simply available.

## From uniform to identity

For most of football's history a shirt was work clothing. It was made of heavy cotton or wool, it belonged to the club, and if you were a supporter it didn't occur to you to wear one. Numbers weren't even standard in the English game until the Football League made them compulsory in 1939, six years after the two teams in the 1933 FA Cup final had worn them, numbered from 1 to 22.

Change arrived from two directions. In 1973 Eintracht Braunschweig put a Jägermeister logo on their chests, one of the moves that opened European football to shirt sponsorship. From then on a shirt was also a piece of advertising, and it would date quickly and precisely. At around the same time, manufacturers began selling replica kits in earnest. Supporters could now buy a version of what the players wore, and clubs soon discovered that they could change it regularly.

That combination, commercial and frequently updated, is usually held up as what is wrong with modern kits. It's also exactly what makes them work as memory. A shirt that changes every year is a calendar. Its sponsor, its collar and the shape of its sleeves can place you in a specific year more accurately than most photographs.

## The biography in the fabric

A worn football shirt is also an unusually personal record. It holds the size you were at fifteen. There may be a faded name printed on the back, chosen by a younger person with firm opinions about who would turn out to be important. It may carry a stain whose origin is known to exactly two people.

This is why the question of value in old shirts is so hard to settle. Collectors will tell you what matters: the rarity, the condition, whether it's match-worn. But most of the shirts people actually keep are worth almost nothing on those terms, and nobody is planning to sell them anyway.

Museums have reached the same conclusion from the other side. The displays at the National Football Museum in Manchester include a Manchester United women's shirt from the 1980s and an England shirt worn by Sue Lopez in the 1970s. Their importance has little to do with design. They're evidence that this football happened at a time when it received very little attention. In the women's game especially, a surviving shirt can do the work that a mountain of newspaper coverage does elsewhere.

## Outside the stadium

The other part of the story is that shirts escaped. Worn with jeans or under a jacket, they've been absorbed into everyday clothes, and they've travelled to places with no connection to the crest on the chest. A shirt seen on a street thousands of miles from its club may be saying something about a player, a colour, a family member who lived abroad, or simply about a stall where it was cheap. There isn't one correct reading.

Clubs and brands have noticed, and the appetite for reissues and archive designs is now a commercial category in its own right. That's fine as far as it goes, but a remake can only copy the design. It can't reproduce the reason someone kept the original.

## The long afterlife

A football season lasts about ten months. The results go into the record books and most of the matches are forgotten. The shirt goes in a drawer, and then one day, decades later, somebody opens the drawer.

Few objects in sport are so ordinary and last so long. They hold on to a year, a place, a size, and a version of the person who bought them. Designed to be replaced, they turn out to be what remains.
