Professional football is designed down to the corner flag. Somebody chose the typeface on the advertising boards, the shade of the seats and the exact green of the grass as it appears on television. Grassroots football has no such person. It looks the way it looks because of what was available, what was left over and what the council happened to paint.

That accident is why it photographs so well. Stand on any park pitch on a Sunday morning and the elements are the same ones that fill a modern stadium, but stripped back and set at odd angles to each other. Two goals. A rectangle of white line, slightly crooked. Twenty-two people dressed in something close to two colours. Behind them, instead of a stand, whatever happens to be there: poplars, pylons, a block of flats, a dog walker who has no intention of moving.

## A palette nobody picked

Start with colour. A professional kit is the result of months of meetings. An amateur team's kit is the result of a bag. Shirts come from three different seasons, so the red has drifted, and one player's socks are a near miss. Someone has forgotten their shorts. The substitute's training top is the wrong blue altogether, and the goalkeeper, who supplies their own gear, belongs to a different visual universe.

Then there are the bibs. Nothing in football is less considered than a mesh bib, and few things are more recognisable. Fluorescent orange or yellow, washed to a chalky pastel and pulled over a sweatshirt, a bib tells you more about the level of a game than any scoreboard. It's a uniform that nobody has ever wanted to wear, and that everyone who has played the game has worn.

The ground supplies the rest. In a wet English November the pitch is less a green than a set of greens, darkest in the goalmouths where the turf has gone. On a baked pitch in summer it fades to straw. In much of the world there's no grass to fade at all, and the surface is red earth or grey dust that the ball lifts into the air on every bounce.

<PullQuote>Nobody chose any of it, which is exactly why it looks so sure of itself.</PullQuote>

## Signs of use

The grassroots game writes on everything. Clubhouse doors carry stencilled names. Boards list officials and fixtures in lettering that started as a straight line and ran out of room. A sponsor's panel, painted by the sponsor's nephew, advertises a garage that closed years ago. Graphic designers pay good money to imitate this kind of unselfconscious lettering. Here, it's just how you tell people where the changing rooms are.

The pitch itself is a drawing, and a surprisingly fragile one. Lines are remade by hand each week with a small wheeled marker, so they wander. Worn patches record exactly where the game happens most: down the middle, in front of each goal, along whichever touchline the wingers favour. Read a pitch at the end of a season and you could almost reconstruct how the team played.

## The light at eleven o'clock

Then there's the hour. Most top-level football is staged in the evening under floodlights, in even white light with four neat shadows at every player's feet. Amateur football happens in daylight, at the hour the pitch was booked for. In winter that means a low sun that throws long shadows across the whole field and turns every breath white. It is unflattering to almost nothing.

That's part of why Hackney Marshes in east London, where the first two photographs in this piece were taken on a late November morning in 2021, has become almost a shorthand for the amateur game in Britain. It's flat and exposed, and it offers little except sky, bare trees and football. Remove the crowd, the branding and the architecture, and what's left is the shape of the game.

## Worth looking at

None of this needs romanticising. Amateur football is often cold and poorly drained, and its facilities are under constant threat. The people who keep it going would, on the whole, prefer a better changing room to a better photograph.

But it deserves to be looked at properly, and the reason isn't nostalgia. The look of the grassroots game is an honest record of how football is actually organised by most of the people who play it: by volunteers, on shared land, with whatever's in the bag. Everything else in the sport's visual culture, from the retro shirts to the concrete-and-floodlight imagery in the latest campaign, is borrowing from it. It's useful to remember where the original is kept.

It's a field near you, around eleven o'clock on Sunday.
