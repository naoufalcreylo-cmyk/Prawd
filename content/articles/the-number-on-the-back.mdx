Hanging in the museum at San Siro, the red and black shirt worn by Kaká has its number enormous across the back: a pair of pale twos. Twenty-two isn't a traditional number for an attacking midfielder. For a while in Milan, it was one of the best-known numbers in the game.

For most of football's history, that wouldn't have made any sense. Numbers were simply a practical label. In the English game they became compulsory in 1939, and for decades they went from 1 to 11 according to where you played. The right back wore 2. If you were picked at centre forward, you wore 9, and on another day someone else would wear it.

## Choosing your own

The change that matters came with fixed squad numbers. The Premier League introduced them in 1993-94, along with names on the back, and other leagues adopted similar systems. A number now belonged to a player for a season, or longer.

Almost immediately, numbers began to mean more than position. Players could pick their own, based on birthdays, superstition, or whichever one a hero had worn. High numbers, once a sign that you were a long way from the first team, started to look like a statement. Number-and-name sets became one of the main things supporters paid for when they bought a shirt.

## Keeping it empty

The strangest stage of this story is a number that no one wears at all. Clubs have retired shirts to honour players. AC Milan, whose shirts fill the display above, retired Paolo Maldini's number 3 after he stopped playing in 2009.

So the same two digits that once said only "right back" can now stand for a whole career. It's a remarkable amount of meaning to ask of something that started as a way to help the people in the stand work out who had the ball.
