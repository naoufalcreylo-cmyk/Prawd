Before kick-off, the most ambitious visual work in football is often happening behind one of the goals. Supporters' groups plan displays for weeks, raising money, cutting and painting sheets in borrowed warehouses and agreeing who holds what. The result lasts a few minutes.

<Figure id="frankfurt-tifo" />

Above, the North West Stand at Eintracht Frankfurt welcomes the 2007/08 Bundesliga season with a cartoon eagle and the club's name, made from thousands of coloured panels. No individual in the stand can see the image they're helping to make. It only exists from the other side of the ground, or in a photograph.

## Colour by the block

Tifo is a strangely strict art form. Each club has its own colours and those are generally all you get. Designs are worked out so they can be read from far away, with a graphic bluntness that owes more to posters than to painting. Banners are lettered big and plain. The audience is thousands of people, across a pitch, in poor light.

## The smoke problem

Then there's the smoke. Pyrotechnics are banned in many football grounds and for good reason: flares burn hot, they injure people, and clubs are regularly fined for them. They're also, undeniably, why many of the most arresting pictures of supporter culture look the way they do, with a whole stand dissolving in red.

Photographers face an awkward bind here, and so do publications. It's possible to find an image beautiful without pretending that what's in it is safe. The pictures on this page are records of what took place, including at Austrian and Polish grounds in the 2000s and 2010s. They aren't recommendations.

## Gone by half-time

What survives afterwards? Almost nothing physical. Card panels end up under seats or in bins. Banners go back to a lock-up, and the smoke clears within minutes. What remains is the picture, which is one reason these images circulate so widely among supporters themselves.

It's an odd way to make something. People spend weeks on it, and they make it for one match, in one stadium, on one afternoon. Then the referee blows the whistle and everybody turns to look at something else.
