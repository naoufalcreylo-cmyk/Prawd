From above, the court at Los Tigres in Punitaqui, in Chile's Coquimbo Region, doesn't look like a sports facility at all. It looks like a topographic map, with swirling bands of colour from orange to turquoise spreading out across the surface. The work is by the La Serena artist Luis Cáceres, and was made in December 2021 for the commune's health department, to promote healthy lives and sport among local children.

It's a good place to start any discussion of football's creative communities, because it's so far from what the phrase usually brings to mind. It isn't a collaboration with a sportswear brand, and there's no launch event. An artist, a local authority and a piece of concrete have together made somewhere children might actually want to play.

## From the bottom up

The creative life of football tends to get reported from the top: a designer's capsule collection, a campaign directed by somebody famous, a photographer's exhibition. All of that is real. But there's far more going on lower down, and much of it is done by people who wouldn't call it art.

<Figure id="dalymount-street-art" />

Outside Dalymount Park in Dublin, home of Bohemian FC, the walls carry painted skulls, the slogan "Love football, hate racism", and the club's name in layers that have built up over time. It's hard to say where official signage ends and the supporters' contribution begins, and it may be better not to try. The result looks like the club because the people around the club made it.

## Small gestures

Even smaller work counts. On the wooden fence around the stadium at Hostinné in the Czech Republic, cartoon footballers have been painted beside a school's name. It's a modest thing, but somebody took the time to do it, and it has stayed up.

<Figure id="hostinne-fence" />

Then there's the stuff that's barely there at all. Football stickers and stencils cover the back of many road signs in many cities. In one example, a cat's head tops a figure in a black and white striped football shirt. It serves no purpose beyond the pleasure of its existence, and it isn't really about any one club. It's somebody making a small joke in public for whoever happens to walk past.

## Community, properly speaking

"Community" has become one of those words that football's marketing departments wear out. Real creative communities are less tidy than the word suggests. They contain artists with commissions and teenagers with spray cans, committee members who paint a fence because it needs doing, and supporters who run off a thousand stickers and never tell anyone.

What links them is that they don't really treat football as content. It's just the place where they live and the thing they care about. So they do what people have always done with such things, and decorate it.
