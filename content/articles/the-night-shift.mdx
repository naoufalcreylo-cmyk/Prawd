From above, on an August night in 2024, The Peninsula Stadium in Salford looks barely attached to the planet. Everything around it has dropped into black, and what's left is one bright green rectangle.

It's a very modern-looking picture of a very old idea. In October 1878, a match was played under electric lights at Bramall Lane in Sheffield, with lamps mounted on towers and powered by generators. It was a novelty, and a popular one. Then the sport more or less left the idea alone for decades.

## A long wait

English football's authorities weren't convinced. The Football Association barred its clubs from playing competitive matches under lights in 1930, and it wasn't until the early 1950s that the position really began to change. The first Football League game under floodlights came on 22 February 1956, when Portsmouth played Newcastle United at Fratton Park.

Once allowed, the lights changed the rhythm of the whole game. Midweek evening kick-offs meant that people could go to a match after work. Clubs could play in midwinter without racing the sunset. European competition, with its Wednesday nights and its sense of occasion, depended on them.

## Light as architecture

Floodlights also gave grounds a new shape on the skyline. Four tall pylons, one at each corner, could tell you where the ground was from miles away. Many have now gone, replaced by neat rows along the stand roofs, and supporters of some clubs still speak fondly about the old towers.

What the lights did to the picture may be their biggest legacy. Under floodlights the pitch is always a vivid green, and the crowd beyond it falls away into shadow. That's the version of football most of us carry around in our heads: not a field in daylight, but a bright stage at night.
