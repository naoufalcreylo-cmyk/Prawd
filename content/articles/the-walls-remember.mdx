On Rumbach Sebestyén utca in central Budapest, a whole side of a building is given over to a football match played more than seventy years ago. The mural commemorates the "Match of the Century", the game at Wembley in November 1953 when Hungary beat England 6-3. Enlarged to the height of several floors, the image has the grain and tone of an old newspaper page, and it includes an oversized leather ball in the foreground.

It's a strange thing to see from the pavement. Most sporting achievements fade into statistics. This one has been given a wall, in a street where people carry on with their day beneath it.

## Why paint it?

Football murals have become common in many cities, and they aren't all alike. Some celebrate a current star, and date within a couple of years. The more interesting ones make a claim about who a place is.

<Figure id="sheffield-snuff-mill-mural" />

On Snuff Mill Lane, a mural for Sheffield FC in red and black declares its subject "The World's First Football Club", with a crest bearing the date 1857. Sheffield FC, founded that year, is widely recognised as the oldest existing football club in the world. It has never been among the sport's major powers, and it doesn't need to be. The painting isn't there to record a trophy. It is telling you that all of this started here.

In Łódź, a mural on Kazimierza Street marks forty years since Widzew Łódź's first Polish league title, won in 1981. It uses a blown-up black-and-white match image, so it reads from a distance like a giant photograph. A club's greatest period is literally kept in public view for residents who may never have seen it first-hand.

## Whose wall is it?

Who paints these things, and who pays for them, matters. Some are commissioned by clubs or sponsors and sit fairly comfortably alongside the marketing. Others begin with supporters, or with the kind of unofficial agreement between an artist and a building's owner that doesn't survive much scrutiny.

The difference tends to show. At their best, walls like these feel as if they've emerged from the neighbourhood. At their worst, they're advertising with better manners. Outside ADO Den Haag's stadium, a large mural in the club's green and yellow, with the stork from its crest, sits squarely at the point where those two things meet. It's official, cheerful and intended for the crowds going in, and there's nothing wrong with that.

## A public archive

What murals share is a commitment to being seen by people who didn't ask. A football museum requires a ticket and a decision to go. A wall just stands there, on your way to work, reminding you of 1953.

They don't last forever. Buildings get repainted or demolished, and weather gets into everything. But for as long as they stay up, they do something the record books can't. They make a piece of football history part of the street.
