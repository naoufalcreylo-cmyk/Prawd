Most football photographs are taken by a small number of accredited professionals from a narrow strip of ground beside the pitch. Their pictures are extraordinary, and they show a tiny fraction of the football played on any given day.

The rest usually goes unrecorded, but not always. Wikimedia Commons, the media library behind Wikipedia, holds a vast, loosely organised collection of images uploaded by volunteers and released for anyone to reuse. Search it for football and, past the stadium tours, you find something unexpected: an accidental atlas of the game. All the photographs in this feature come from there, and each credit names the person who took it.

<Figure id="totoralillo-beach" />

Nobody commissioned this archive, and it has no editor in the usual sense. Photographers file their pictures with short, plain descriptions. "Kids from south (desert) playing football/ soccer!" "A football pitch in the village of Vogjani." "Young people enjoy football (soccer) on the sand in front of the local church." The language is closer to a catalogue than a caption. It leaves you to look.

## The surface decides

What stands out across hundreds of these images is the ground. A photographer at a professional match could be anywhere, since one perfect pitch looks much like another. Here, the surface is the subject. Red earth in Africa, beach sand on the coast of Chile, a dry field in a village in North Macedonia: each changes how the ball moves and how the players stand, and what the picture looks like.

<Figure id="vogjani-pitch" />

The horizon changes too. A stadium is built to shut out the world, but these pitches are open to it. There are mountains and rooftops behind the goal, a church tower or the edge of the sea, and very often no goal at all, just two markers and an agreement.

## Who gets seen

The archive also records people who have seldom been the subject of football photography. One contributor uploaded a match between women and girls at a Women's Day celebration in Muguluka, in Jinja, Uganda, and noted drily that the women "obviously beat the girls 1-0". Another shows a girl taking part in practice with boys. These images aren't trying to make an argument. They record who was on the pitch that day, and that's a contribution in itself.

## An archive of attention

An archive this loose has obvious limits. It's uneven, and it depends entirely on who has a camera, the time and the inclination to upload. Its descriptions can be sketchy and its coverage has huge gaps. Nor can it tell you much about the lives of the people in the frame.

But it gets something right that polished football imagery rarely does. It shows how ordinary the game is, in the best sense: a thing people do, everywhere, whether or not anyone is watching. Then, every so often, someone is watching. They take a picture, and they give it away.
