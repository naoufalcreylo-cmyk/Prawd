*The speaker is invented. Every amateur club relies on someone like them, and few of its players could tell you their surname.*

<Q>How did you end up looking after the kit?</Q>

My son played for the under-14s. One week nobody had washed the shirts, so I took them home. That was nineteen years ago. He stopped playing in about 2011.

<Q>What does the job actually involve?</Q>

A washing machine and a very strong back. Two teams, sometimes three. There are shirts, shorts, socks and bibs every week, more in winter, because in winter they come back weighing twice as much. I go through the pockets, and I've found phones, keys and a set of false teeth. I've never found out whose.

<Q>What's the most common crisis?</Q>

Socks. A bag goes out with fifteen pairs and comes back with thirteen and a half. There's no explanation. It isn't theft, as nobody would want them. It's more like a natural process. Then there's number nine, because everyone wants number nine, and it's got a hole in it.

<Q>Do you have favourites among the shirts?</Q>

I shouldn't, should I? But there's an old set from the 2000s we still keep for cup games. It's terrible material and doesn't fit anyone, and the sponsor went bust years ago. But the lads who wore it still come back to watch, and they like to see it. I've mended those shirts more times than I've mended anything of my own.

<Q>Does the kit bag give you any power at the club?</Q>

More than the chairman. He can make a speech. But if a player has been messing me around, it's amazing how often the only shirt left is the small one.

<Q>Has anyone ever thanked you?</Q>

They gave me a card once, and a bottle of something. It's all right. Nobody thanks the washing machine either, but you notice when it stops working.
