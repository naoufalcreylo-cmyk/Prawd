In the exhibition *Pasión de Multitudes* at the National Historical Museum of Argentina, football shirts hang in niches set into green walls, among hats, trophies and other relics. There's a section for the men's game and another for the women's. The setting is one normally reserved for presidents and generals. A country's football has been placed, quite deliberately, alongside its history.

The first thing a museum does to a shirt is stop it moving. A football shirt is an object made for motion and sweat, and it's designed to look its best at a sprint. On a mannequin it goes still. Your eye drifts to what you'd never otherwise notice: the stitching, the thin cotton, how small some of the players were.

## Who gets a mannequin?

The second thing a museum does is confer importance, and that raises the question of who receives it. At the San Siro museum in Milan, two blue Italy shirts stand side by side, worn by Giacinto Facchetti and Sandro Mazzola. It's a very traditional sort of display, with great players, great shirts and a strong sense of reverence. Most football museums began this way, with the sport's famous names as a kind of secular saints.

<Figure id="san-siro-italy-shirts" />

Newer displays have been widening the circle. A women's section in a national museum in Argentina is a statement in its own right, and so are the displays elsewhere that recover the shirts of players who were given little recognition at the time. In these cases the display is doing some of the work that was neglected in the first place.

## What the glass takes away

Something is always lost. Behind glass a shirt can no longer be touched or worn, and it has no smell. It no longer has anything to do with the person who bought a replica, stood in the rain, and still has it in a drawer. There's a strange distance in looking at something so familiar with the respect you'd give to an archaeological find.

It may be why the crowd at a good football museum seems so talkative. People point and tell each other stories, and they say that they had that one. They seem to be trying to get the object back out through the glass. Very few museums can claim that kind of engagement.
