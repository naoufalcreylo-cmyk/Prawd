Look at an aerial photograph of almost any settlement in Britain and you can usually find the football pitch within a few seconds. It's the only thing down there with straight edges and nothing on it. Houses crowd up to one side. A hedge or a car park holds the other. In the middle, somebody has left a large rectangle of land empty, on purpose, and kept it that way for decades.

That emptiness is the point. The photograph at the top of this page shows the sports pitch and village hall at Stonesfield in Oxfordshire. In a place like this, the pitch has few rivals as a shared space. Very few other open spaces are held in common, and very few gather people who wouldn't otherwise have a reason to stand next to each other.

## Land that says something

Dedicating land to a game is a surprisingly strong statement. Open ground near houses is valuable, and it comes under pressure from every direction. For a pitch to survive, it needs someone to mow it, to mark it, to book it and to argue for it at meetings, season after season. The fact that a pitch is still there usually means that a small number of people have been quietly refusing to let it go.

Those people tend not to think of themselves as custodians of public space. They think of themselves as the person with the keys. But what they look after does a job that very little else in a small town can do. A pub excludes the under-18s, and a church or school gate brings together only part of a village. A Saturday game has room for grandparents, children, the bored teenagers on the wall, and people who've come along only because their son needed a lift.

## Measuring time

A local pitch also keeps the time. Many people can place themselves there at every stage of their life: playing as a child on a muddy Saturday, watching their own children, standing on the same touchline decades later and recognising the surname of the left back. In a place where shops close and families move on, this kind of continuity is rare.

The clubhouse often keeps the other half of the record. Team photographs go up on its walls, a season at a time, until they run out of space. It's easy to spot the same faces moving through them, from the back row of the youth team to the far end of the committee.

## Beyond the white line

It's easy to get sentimental about all this, and to pretend that local football has only ever been welcoming. It hasn't. Plenty of people have felt unwelcome at pitches that were meant to belong to everyone, and many clubs have had to work hard, and sometimes be pushed, to change that. Being part of a place means being part of its arguments too.

But the arguments are also evidence that it matters. People don't fight over a space that means nothing. When a pitch is threatened by a new development, or a change of use, or a slow lack of money, the opposition tends to come from far beyond the people who actually play on it. Residents who've never kicked a ball there turn out to have strong feelings about it. They may walk the dog across it each morning, or they may simply like knowing it's there.

## Keep the gap

Good towns need gaps, and the football pitch is one of the few that comes with a reason to look after it. Its value doesn't show up on a map, where it looks like nothing much, a pale rectangle waiting to be filled.

Walk down on a Saturday, though, and you'll find the whole place in one line along the touchline, with a hot drink, complaining about the referee.
