The display case at Wroxham Miniature Worlds in Norfolk is a very green place. There are boxed teams, tiny floodlights, a curved section of stand and a stack of accessories, all arranged behind glass. For a certain generation, it's the most evocative thing in the building.

Subbuteo was launched in Britain in the years after the Second World War by Peter Adolph. He wanted to call it The Hobby, but that wasn't allowed. So he turned to the Latin name of the hobby falcon, *Falco subbuteo*, and so a bird became one of the best-known names in British toys.

## A world in miniature

What made the game work wasn't only the flicking. It was the completeness. You could buy the teams, of course, but also the stands, the crowd, the television camera tower and the ball boys. A real ground was an unreachable, expensive, adult place. This one fitted on a dining table, and you were in charge of it.

It also taught people to look at kits with unusual care. Hand-painted figures reduced each team to its most basic colours and patterns, and to a child those simple versions could be more vivid than the real thing.

## The table in the corner

Table football is a different branch of the family, sturdier and noisier. The old wooden game above sits in the Musée des jeux traditionnels in Loon-Plage, in northern France. It is dark with age, with small figures lined up across its playing surface and a metal plate fixed to the front.

Whether it's flicked with a finger or spun on a rod, football at this scale keeps one important quality of the real game. There's always someone who takes it far too seriously, and very often it's you.
