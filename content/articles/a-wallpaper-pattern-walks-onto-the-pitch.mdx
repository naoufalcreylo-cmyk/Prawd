{/* section: the-design */}

Most football shirts look outward, to whichever template the manufacturer is offering this season. This one looked closer to home.

The blue surface adapts "Yare", a pattern designed around 1892 by John Henry Dearle for Morris & Co. The original was a block print on cotton, made for interiors. According to the Victoria and Albert Museum, which holds one of the shirts in its collection, this version is printed on polyester by sublimation, where heat and pressure transfer the design into the fabric.

The two processes could hardly be further apart, yet the pattern survives the move well. Morris & Co. designs were built to repeat across large areas without losing their rhythm, and that's essentially what a shirt needs too. At a distance it reads as a textured blue. Up close, it opens out into leaves and stems.

Football has seen plenty of all-over patterns since digital printing made them cheap, and many are attractive without meaning much. This one has a reason to be there, and that reason lies in the next tab.

{/* section: the-place */}

William Morris spent part of his youth in Walthamstow, at Water House on Forest Road. The building is now the William Morris Gallery, and that fact is what makes the shirt more than a clever piece of decoration.

Plenty of clubs can reference a local landmark. Few have a neighbour whose name is known wherever textile design is studied. For a shirt worn in non-league football, the pattern works in two directions at once: it's a piece of international design history, and it's also just something from down the road.

That's the advantage a small club has over a global one. A brand serving supporters on several continents has to stay broad. A club in east London can be precise, and the more precise it is, the less anyone else can copy it.

{/* section: the-people */}

The V&A's record credits the original concept to Mark Clack, founder of the Walthamstow design collective Wood Street Walls. The club, the gallery and Admiral then took it on, with the gallery providing access to its archive. The record describes it as a "first-of-its-kind collaboration between a football club and a museum".

The shirt shows who was involved. The William Morris Gallery appears on the front alongside the club and Admiral. The sleeves carry Wood Street Walls and No Space for Hate. Urban by Nature and Exhale Brewery are on the reverse. It's closer to a list of neighbours than a commercial sponsorship in the usual sense.

The photograph above isn't of anyone involved in the project. It's a fabric shop on Walthamstow Market, a reminder that cloth is still bought and sold by the metre not far from where the pattern's history is kept.

{/* section: the-supporters */}

The V&A records that the shirts were released in July 2023 and sold out within days, and that sales were intended to help fund a women's team in Waltham Forest.

That's what can be documented. It's worth being clear about what can't. PRAWD hasn't interviewed supporters for this demonstration story, so we won't tell you what the shirt means to them or put words in their mouths. A quick sell-out doesn't reveal who bought it. Some will have been regulars at the club, others design fans, others curious people who had never seen a match there.

So this is the angle that a finished version of this story would need to report properly. Who stands on the touchline wearing it now? Did the shirt bring anyone through the gate who hadn't been before? Does it feel like theirs, or like something made about them? The rest of the story is about an object. This part can only be answered by people.
