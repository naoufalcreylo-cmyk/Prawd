The goal at the Miladinov Brothers Primary School in the village of Kališta, in North Macedonia, is a plain white frame. Someone has planted small conifers and flowers along the front of it, where a stray shot could flatten them at any moment. Behind it are rows of crops and, further off, the hills and houses of the valley.

The picture is one of a long series uploaded to Wikimedia Commons by the volunteer photographer Kiril Simeonovski, whose files document schools and village sports grounds around the country. They aren't presented as football pictures. They're a record of what stands where. But for anyone interested in how the game fits into daily life, they're a remarkable set.

## A surface for everything

School-yard pitches have a hard life. They're also netball courts, assembly points and places to stand during fire drills. At the school in Gorno Tateši, a rain-wet court holds a sheet of reflected sky, with a tall fence for keeping balls out of the neighbours' gardens. At the school in Sušica, a bright red court with crisp white lines looks almost too clean for what it's bound to go through.

These aren't surfaces anyone would consider good for the game, and they've probably skinned a lot of knees. They do have one big advantage, though. They're there every day, a few metres from a classroom, and they're free to use. For children in a small village, this is where football happens first, with no club to join and nothing to book.

## The size of the world

Everyone who plays football remembers a pitch like this one, and remembers it as being much larger than it was. Those first few metres of tarmac or dirt set the scale for the game in your head. It's where you found out about the wall behind the goal, the drain you could use as a marker, and the kid who wouldn't pass.

The flowers by the goal at Kališta won't feature in any coaching manual. But somebody decided that a school football pitch was worth planting flowers beside, and that's a good enough reason to keep the picture.
